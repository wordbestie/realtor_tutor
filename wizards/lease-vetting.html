Vetting a Lease Application

Screen a prospective tenant the right way — application, income, credit, and references — then summarize it cleanly for your landlord to decide.

Step 1 of 5

Collect a Complete Application

Vetting starts with a full picture of the applicant. Before you assess anything, make sure you actually have everything in hand.

  • Confirm the rental application is fully completed — current address, employment, and rental history all filled in.
  • Collect government-issued photo ID and note that names match across every document.
  • Gather contact details for the applicant’s employer, current landlord, and personal references.
  • Note Only collect what you genuinely need to assess the tenancy, and handle every document in line with privacy obligations.
Step 2 of 5

Verify Income & Employment

Confirm the applicant can comfortably afford the rent.

  • Request recent pay stubs (typically the last two to three) or a signed letter of employment stating role, start date, and salary.
  • For self-employed applicants, ask for recent tax assessments or bank statements showing consistent income.
  • As a rough guideline, many landlords look for monthly income around three times the rent — note where the applicant lands.
  • Tip Call the employer to confirm the letter is genuine, rather than relying on the document alone.
Step 3 of 5

Review Credit

A credit check shows how the applicant has handled obligations in the past.

  • Obtain a credit report with the applicant’s written consent.
  • Look at the score, any history of late payments or collections, and overall debt load relative to income.
  • Treat a weaker score in context — a strong income, references, or a co-signer can offset it.
  • Note You must have the applicant’s explicit consent before pulling credit, and you should not over-rely on score alone.
Step 4 of 5

Check References

References fill in the human side that documents can’t.

  • Call the current or previous landlord: was rent paid on time, was the unit kept in good condition, would they rent to this person again?
  • Speak with the employer to confirm stable, ongoing employment.
  • Use personal references sparingly — they’re a softer signal than landlord and employer checks.
  • Tip Ask open-ended questions and listen for hesitation; a quick “no comment” can be as telling as the answer.
Step 5 of 5

Summarize for Your Landlord

Pull it all together into a clear, even-handed summary so your landlord can make the call.

  • Lay out the key facts: income vs. rent, credit highlights, and the outcome of each reference call.
  • Flag any concerns honestly, alongside any mitigating factors (co-signer, larger deposit where permitted, strong references).
  • Present the applicant’s strengths and risks neutrally — the decision is the landlord’s to make.
  • Note Apply the same criteria to every applicant to stay consistent and within human-rights and fair-housing rules.
